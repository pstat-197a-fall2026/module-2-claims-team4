HTML scraping

Yes, including header content with paragraph text improves classification performance greatly across all evaluation metrics. Comparing word-level principal component logistic regression (PCR) on the shared 70/10/20 split, adding header content increases test accuracy from 0.551899 to 0.77722, increases ROC AUC from 0.543552 to 0.84167, and decreases test log-loss from 0.685575 to 0.52701. (These results can all be seen in ‘/results/prelim-task1-shared-split.csv’).

Additionally, these improvements are consistent across all 10 stratified random splits. The accuracy mean increased from 0.627848 to 0.70329, accuracy standard deviation decreased from 0.10370 to 0.09902. The ROC AUC mean increased from 0.618695 to 0.77742, ROC AUC standard deviation decreased from 0.17511 to 0.074134. Finally, the log-loss mean decreased from 0.67156 to 0.584486, and the log-loss standard deviation stayed around the same from 0.0568376 to 0.0587766. All these three pairs of results show that since the means are increasing, classification performance is improving, and since the standard deviations are decreasing, the classification performance is more precise and reliable. (These results can all be seen in ‘/results/prelim-task1-summary.csv’)

Bigrams

No, bigrams do not capture meaningful, additional information beyond unigram word features.

We tested whether bigrams added meaningful information using a two-stage stacked logistic regression on the combined paragraph and header text (“ph”). In Stage 1, we fit a word-level TF-IDF PCR model to generate out-of-fold predicted log-odds on the training set, and a full-fit log-odds on the test set.

In Stage 2, we fit a logistic regression combining Stage 1 word log-odds with the top \(m\) principal components from a bigram TF-IDF matrix, which was filtered to bigrams appearing in at least 5 documents, selecting \(m \in \{0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 35, 50, 75, 100\}\) via 5-fold cross-validated log-loss and comparing against word-only PCR, recalibrated word-only PCR (\(m = 0\)), and bigrams-only PCR baselines across the shared split and 9 additional random splits.

On the shared split, cross-validation selected \(m = 0\) bigram PCs, showing that the stacked model reduced identically to recalibrated word-only PCR (test accuracy = 0.7949, AUC = 0.8417, log-loss = 0.5680), while a bigram-only PCR performed substantially worse (test accuracy = 0.7013, AUC = 0.7369, log-loss = 0.6998). Across all 10 splits, adding bigram PCs produced basically no change over recalibrated word-only PCR.

Neural net

We developed and compared five neural network configurations on the combined header-and-paragraph text using our shared 70/10/20 data split to identify an optimal architecture for webpage classification. Our best-performing design converts each page into its 5,000 most frequent single-word features, passes them through a single 32-unit non-linear (ReLU) hidden layer paired with dropout and weight penalties to prevent memorizing noise, and outputs a final class probability through a sigmoid layer. For optimization and loss, the network was trained using the Adam optimization algorithm to minimize binary cross-entropy loss, which directly penalizes inaccurate probability estimates. Across training epochs, we set a maximum limit of 40 passes through the training data with an automated early-stopping rule monitoring our validation set and our winning model converged and stopped after 8 epochs, automatically restoring the weights from its best validation pass. In terms of predictive accuracy, this compact network achieved 84.8% test accuracy (and 0.882 AUC), outperforming Task 1’s principal component regression benchmark (77.7% accuracy) by 7.1 percentage points. While expanding the architecture to a wider 64-unit layer (80.0% accuracy, 10 epochs) or a deeper two-layer network (60.3% accuracy, 6 epochs) reduced performance due to overfitting on single words, feeding two-word phrases (bigrams) into the 64-unit architecture (13 epochs) rebounded test accuracy to 84.6%.
